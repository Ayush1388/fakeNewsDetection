1. The Meta-Learner Weights (shap_stacking_weights.png)
This bar chart visualizes the brain of your Level-2 Stacking Ensemble.

In your pipeline, the Stacking Ensemble does not look at the raw text. Instead, it looks at the probabilities generated by your three Level-1 base models (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and Naive Bayes) and decides which one to trust. This chart shows exactly how much weight the XGBoost meta-learner assigned to each of those base models.

RandomForest_Prob: This is the most powerful signal in your ensemble. With a mean SHAP value of roughly 6.0, the meta-learner learned that the Random Forest's predictions were by far the most reliable. When the Random Forest makes a confident prediction, the ensemble heavily defers to it.

LogReg_Prob: Logistic Regression is the second most trusted model (SHAP value ~1.9). This shows that the linear mathematical patterns found by Logistic Regression provided a solid, secondary "second opinion" that the meta-learner used to fine-tune the final guess.

NaiveBayes_Prob: This model has the lowest impact (~0.5). Because Naive Bayes assumes all words are independent of each other (which is rarely true in human language), the meta-learner learned to largely ignore its predictions in favor of the other two models.

What to tell your professor: "This chart proves that our Stacking Ensemble is functioning correctly. Instead of just blindly averaging the three base models, the meta-learner intelligently learned to prioritize the non-linear decision trees of the Random Forest while using Logistic Regression as a stabilizing baseline."

2. The Feature Impact Summary (shap_xgboost_summary.png)
This is a SHAP Beeswarm Plot for your standalone XGBoost model. It is the most important image in your project because it proves that your custom feature engineering actually worked.

Here is how to read it:

The X-Axis: Shows the SHAP value. Anything pushed to the Right (Positive > 0) means the model is classifying the article as Fake News. Anything pushed to the Left (Negative < 0) means the model is classifying it as Real News.

The Color: Represents the actual value of the feature in the dataset. Red means the feature was high (e.g., a high Flesch score). Blue means the feature was low.

Here is what the model actually learned about fake news based on the data points:

burstiness_std (Sentence Variance): Notice how all the bright red dots are stretched far to the right, and the blue dots are on the left. This proves that articles with high sentence length variance (highly erratic, emotional writing) are strongly predicted as Fake News. Consistent, professional writing (blue) is predicted as Real News.

said: The word "said" has its red dots pushed entirely to the left. This is a massive journalistic marker. Real news heavily relies on quoting sources (e.g., "The police chief said..."). If an article frequently uses the word "said", the model strongly predicts it is Real News.

flesch_reading_ease: The red dots are grouped on the right side. This means that a higher Flesch score (which actually translates to an easier, lower grade-level reading requirement) is a strong indicator of Fake News. The model figured out that misinformation is often written at a simpler reading level to appeal to a broader, more susceptible audience.

Artifacts (video, image, featured): These words have red dots pushing to the right. This indicates that the fake news dataset likely contained scraped web formatting text (e.g., "Click here to watch the featured video"), which the model learned to associate with unreliable domains.

What to tell your professor: "This beeswarm plot validates our hypothesis. It proves the model did not just memorize political keywords. It actively learned that structural stylometrics—like erratic sentence burstiness and simplistic reading levels—are the primary mathematical footprints of Fake News, while direct journalistic quoting (the word 'said') is the strongest footprint of Real News."
